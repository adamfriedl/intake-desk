Title: Debt Collection Communications — What Collectors Can and Cannot Do (FTC Public Education)

Debt collectors contact people who owe money to creditors. Sometimes the collector works for the original creditor; sometimes it is a separate debt buyer. The rules depend in part on whether the collector is covered by the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, which generally applies to third-party collectors collecting debts on behalf of others.

Collectors may contact you by phone, mail, email, or text. They must identify themselves as debt collectors in every communication. They must tell you that any information they obtain will be used to collect the debt.

Collectors may not contact you at inconvenient times or places. They generally may not call before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time unless you agree. They may not contact you at work if you tell them your employer prohibits such calls.

Harassment is illegal. Collectors may not use threats of violence, obscene language, or repeated calls intended to annoy. They may not publish lists of people who refuse to pay debts. They may not misrepresent the character, amount, or legal status of a debt.

Collectors may not lie. They may not falsely claim to be attorneys or government representatives. They may not threaten arrest for ordinary consumer debt. They may not tell you legal papers are not legal forms when they are, or vice versa.

You can ask a collector to stop contacting you. If you send a written request to stop contact, the collector may only contact you to confirm there will be no further contact or to notify you of a specific action such as filing a lawsuit. Keep a copy of your letter.

A collection call does not by itself mean you have been sued. A lawsuit usually involves formal service of court papers with response deadlines. Treat summonses separately from collection letters.

If you believe a collector broke the law, document each contact and consider reporting to the FTC, CFPB, and your state attorney general. An advocate can help evaluate whether you have additional remedies.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) generally applies to third-party debt collectors collecting debts owed to others, not always to original creditors collecting their own debts in their own name. Oregon and other states may have additional consumer protection laws covering original creditors. Identifying who is calling—original creditor, assigned collector, or debt buyer—helps determine which rules apply.

Cease communication requests are powerful but narrow. After a written cease letter, the collector may only contact you to confirm cessation or notify you of specific actions like filing suit. They may still sue. Cease letters do not erase valid debts or stop credit reporting by creditors. Send cease requests by certified mail and keep copies.

Validation rights interact with communication rules. Within five days of initial contact, collectors must send a validation notice with amount, creditor name, and dispute instructions. Disputing within 30 days pauses collection until verification is provided. See the debt validation digest for detail.

Third-party disclosure rules limit who collectors may tell about your debt. Collectors generally cannot discuss your debt with employers, neighbors, or family except to locate you, and even location contacts have limits. Repeated calls to your workplace after you said your employer prohibits calls violate educational summaries of FDCPA themes.

Recorded calls and voicemails are evidence. Note date, time, caller name and company, claimed balance, and threatening language. Some states restrict call recording consent rules—Oregon is a one-party consent state for many recordings, but verify before relying on recordings in court.

Debt buyers purchase portfolios of old accounts and may lack complete documentation. That does not automatically mean you owe nothing, but it affects lawsuit defense and validation responses. Account numbers may change; match old statements to new collector letters carefully.

When collectors contact you about multiple accounts, treat each account separately for disputes and payment decisions. A payment on one account does not resolve others unless a written agreement says so.

Escalate for advocate review when: collectors threaten arrest or criminal prosecution for consumer debt, contact you after a written cease letter without permitted exceptions, discuss your debt with unauthorized third parties, or misrepresent themselves as attorneys or government agents. Summons service is always separate high urgency.

Reporting channels include the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov/complaint), Federal Trade Commission, and Oregon Department of Justice consumer protection division. Complaints do not replace lawsuit responses.
