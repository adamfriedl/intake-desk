Title: Debt Collection FAQs — Validation and Your Rights (FTC Public Education)

If you owe money, a debt collector may contact you to collect it. Federal law limits how debt collectors can contact you and what they must tell you.

When a debt collector first contacts you, they must send a written "validation notice" within five days. The notice must include how much you owe, the name of the creditor, and what to do if you do not think you owe the debt.

You have the right to dispute a debt. If you send a dispute letter within 30 days of receiving the validation notice, the collector must stop collection efforts until it sends you verification of the debt. Keep a copy of your letter and send it in a way that gives you proof of delivery.

If you do not recognize the debt, the amount seems wrong, or you think the debt is not yours, dispute it in writing. Do not assume that paying a small amount will make the problem go away without a written agreement about what the payment resolves.

Debt collectors may not harass you. They may not use obscene language, threaten violence, or call repeatedly to annoy you. They may not lie about who they are or misrepresent the amount you owe. They may not tell you that you will be arrested if you do not pay a consumer debt.

Collectors may contact you by phone, mail, email, or text message, but they must follow rules about when and how often they contact you. You can tell a collector to stop contacting you at work if your employer does not allow such calls. You can also send a letter asking the collector to stop all contact except to tell you about specific actions like filing a lawsuit.

If a collector violates the law, you may report the problem to the Federal Trade Commission, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and your state attorney general. You may also have the right to sue in some situations. Documentation — dates, names, amounts, and copies of letters — helps advocates evaluate your options.

This excerpt orients lay users to validation and dispute basics. It does not predict lawsuit outcomes or recommend whether to pay a particular debt.
