Title: Debt Collection FAQs — Validation and Your Rights (FTC Public Education)

If you owe money, a debt collector may contact you to collect it. Federal law limits how debt collectors can contact you and what they must tell you.

When a debt collector first contacts you, they must send a written "validation notice" within five days. The notice must include how much you owe, the name of the creditor, and what to do if you do not think you owe the debt.

You have the right to dispute a debt. If you send a dispute letter within 30 days of receiving the validation notice, the collector must stop collection efforts until it sends you verification of the debt. Keep a copy of your letter and send it in a way that gives you proof of delivery.

If you do not recognize the debt, the amount seems wrong, or you think the debt is not yours, dispute it in writing. Do not assume that paying a small amount will make the problem go away without a written agreement about what the payment resolves.

Debt collectors may not harass you. They may not use obscene language, threaten violence, or call repeatedly to annoy you. They may not lie about who they are or misrepresent the amount you owe. They may not tell you that you will be arrested if you do not pay a consumer debt.

Collectors may contact you by phone, mail, email, or text message, but they must follow rules about when and how often they contact you. You can tell a collector to stop contacting you at work if your employer does not allow such calls. You can also send a letter asking the collector to stop all contact except to tell you about specific actions like filing a lawsuit.

If a collector violates the law, you may report the problem to the Federal Trade Commission, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and your state attorney general. You may also have the right to sue in some situations. Documentation — dates, names, amounts, and copies of letters — helps advocates evaluate your options.

This excerpt orients lay users to validation and dispute basics. It does not predict lawsuit outcomes or recommend whether to pay a particular debt.
Debt validation is the process by which a collector proves you owe a specific amount to a specific creditor. The validation notice must arrive within five days of initial contact in most FDCPA-covered cases. If you dispute in writing within 30 days of receiving that notice, collection must stop until the collector mails verification. Verification is not always a full contract package—it may be account statements and identification of the creditor—but collectors cannot continue collection without responding to a timely dispute.

Disputes after the 30-day window may still be useful for credit reporting and lawsuit defense but may not trigger the same automatic collection pause under federal law. Send disputes anyway if the debt is wrong, with proof of mailing.

Common validation scenarios include: debts you do not recognize because the account was opened fraudulently; balances inflated by fees you never agreed to; debts already paid or discharged in bankruptcy; debts belonging to someone with a similar name; and zombie debts revived beyond the statute of limitations (see statute of limitations digest—payment may restart clocks in some cases).

Dispute letters should identify the account, state why you dispute, and request verification. Do not admit owing the debt in dispute letters unless an advocate advises that strategic admission helps in your situation. Request the name and address of the original creditor and proof of assignment if a debt buyer owns the account.

If validation arrives, read whether it satisfies your dispute. Incomplete verification may support further dispute or complaint. If the collector resumes collection without verification after a timely dispute, document dates for FDCPA complaint themes.

Credit reporting disputes are parallel but separate. Disputing with Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion under the Fair Credit Reporting Act does not replace a collector validation dispute. Both tracks may be needed if inaccurate tradelines appear.

Lawsuit timing: collectors may file suit before or after validation disputes resolve. A pending dispute does not automatically stop a court deadline. Calendar summons response dates independently.

Identity theft debts require police reports, FTC identity theft reports (IdentityTheft.gov), and fraud disputes to collectors and credit bureaus. See the identity theft digest.

Escalate when: validation never arrives after timely dispute but collection continues; sued on a debt you disputed as not yours; collector refuses to identify original creditor; or balance jumps without explanation.

Documentation checklist: validation notice, your dispute letter with mailing proof, all subsequent collector letters, account statements from original creditor if available, and credit report tradelines for the account.
