Title: Prohibited Debt Collection Practices (FTC-Style Education)
Canonical references: https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/

FTC educational materials describe collection practices that can be illegal: repeated calls intended to harass, obscenity, false claims that the collector is a government official or attorney when untrue, threats of arrest for ordinary consumer debt, and disclosing the debt to unauthorized third parties.

Consumers who experience these tactics should document incidents and consider complaints to the FTC, CFPB, and state attorneys general, in addition to seeking legal advice about private remedies. Documentation quality matters more than volume of adjectives.

Threats of arrest for consumer debt are a major red flag. Escalate for advocate review.
Harassment under federal debt collection law includes conduct intended to abuse, oppress, or harass any person. Educational materials list examples: repeated phone calls with intent to annoy; obscene or profane language; publishing debtor lists (except to credit bureaus in permitted ways); and calling without meaningful disclosure of identity.

False or misleading representations are separate violations: claiming to be an attorney when not, misstating the amount owed, threatening actions that cannot legally be taken, and implying documents are not legal forms when they are. Collectors may not threaten wage garnishment before obtaining a judgment except in limited government-debt contexts.

Contact frequency and timing rules complement harassment themes. Calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time are generally prohibited unless you agree. Calls at work after you notify the collector that your employer prohibits them should stop.

Mini-Miranda warnings require collectors to disclose they are attempting to collect a debt and that information obtained will be used for that purpose. Failure to disclose in initial communications supports complaint documentation.

CFPB Regulation F modernized many contact rules including voicemail content, email and text opt-outs, and limits on consecutive contact attempts. Older FTC educational summaries remain directionally correct but pair with current CFPB materials for detail.

Document harassment contemporaneously: keep a call log with date, time, caller ID, representative name, and summary of statements. Save voicemails and texts. Screenshots of caller ID help. Witness statements from household members who overheard calls add support.

Emotional distress alone does not always produce easy remedies, but pattern documentation supports regulatory complaints and some private actions where available. Advocates evaluate whether FDCPA or state law claims are viable.

Harassment red flags requiring escalation: threats of arrest; collectors posing as police; contacts to your employer about the debt beyond location exceptions; calls to relatives repeatedly; or collectors ignoring written cease communication without permitted follow-up.

Do not confuse harassment with legitimate lawsuit service. Process servers and court mail are not "collectors" in the FDCPA sense though they may feel threatening. Respond to court papers on deadline regardless of collector conduct.

Reporting to CFPB and FTC creates administrative records; keep complaint confirmation numbers. State attorney general consumer divisions may also investigate patterns.

This digest supports triage and documentation themes. It does not predict monetary damages or recommend litigation strategy.
Attorney collectors must identify actual law firm and whether attorney reviewed account—misleading attorney branding violates educational summaries of prohibited conduct.

Social media contact about debts to contacts or followers generally prohibited—save screenshots of improper social contact.

Text message frequency after opt-out request documents Reg F violations—note each text timestamp after written opt-out.

Collectors contacting after bankruptcy discharge may violate discharge injunction—notify bankruptcy attorney immediately with collector contact details.

State mini-FDCPA laws in Oregon and elsewhere may cover creditors original collectors excluded from federal FDCPA—ask advocate about Oregon Unlawful Debt Collection Practices Act themes.

Emotional harm documentation supports complaint narratives even when statutory damages uncertain—describe sleep loss, anxiety, and work interference factually without exaggeration.
Reg F limited contact attempts per debt per day—log excessive calls across multiple days for pattern evidence.

Voicemail mini-Miranda and debt details restrictions—collector leaving balance on family answering machine documents violation theme.

Social media shaming about debt prohibited—screenshot posts tagging debtor publicly.
