Title: Eviction Basics in Oregon (Educational)
Canonical references: https://oregonlawhelp.org/

Eviction generally involves a notice stage and, if unresolved, a court stage. Notices communicate the landlord’s claimed reason and timing. Court cases seek a judgment for possession and sometimes money. Tenants should not confuse an informal demand to leave with a court judgment authorizing law enforcement to remove them—but they also should not ignore either.

Common educational themes: read the notice carefully; do not miss court dates; habitability and improper notice can be relevant but are fact-specific; illegal lockouts are improper; settlements should be understood before signing.

Outcomes are never guaranteed. This digest orients a layperson to process vocabulary so intake and retrieval can ground more specific answers in companion sources on notices, FED process, and habitability.
Eviction is a process, not a single event. It typically begins when the landlord delivers a termination notice stating a reason and a deadline to vacate or cure. If the tenant remains past that deadline, the landlord may file a court case seeking possession. Only after court judgment and proper enforcement steps can law enforcement remove a tenant in the ordinary course. Verbal demands, text messages saying "be out Friday," and lock changes without court order are not the final legal eviction—even when they feel like one.

Nonpayment is the most common stated reason, but for-cause and no-cause pathways exist depending on tenancy type and local law. Each pathway has different notice lengths and content requirements. An eviction "for nonpayment" when rent was paid may be defensible with proof. An eviction for lease violation may be curable if the tenant fixes the issue within the notice window.

Tenants sometimes leave voluntarily after receiving a notice to avoid court, which may be practical but is not legally required until a judgment enforces possession. Voluntary move-out without an agreement may still leave deposit disputes and rent claims unresolved. Signed move-out agreements should specify whether the tenant owes anything after surrender.

Court eviction (FED) adds summons service, first appearance, possible trial, judgment, writ, and sheriff enforcement. Each stage has deadlines. Default judgment from missing court is a frequent preventable loss.

Habitability, retaliation, and discrimination can intersect with eviction defenses but require evidence and proper presentation. "The apartment was bad" without documented repair requests rarely carries the same weight as dated photos, code reports, and written complaints.

Rent assistance and diversion programs exist in some Oregon communities during certain funding periods. Eligibility and timing vary; a pending assistance payment rarely stops court automatically without a filed agreement or stay order.

After move-out, whether voluntary or enforced, collections for alleged unpaid rent may continue. Eviction records and credit reporting raise separate long-term housing barriers. Expungement or sealing rules for eviction records change with legislation—verify current Oregon law.

Escalate immediately upon: any court service, lockout, utility shutoff, or notice combined with domestic violence or voucher termination. Medium urgency: notice only, with more than a few days before expiration. Gather lease, notices, payments, and photos before intake calls when possible.

Cash for keys agreements should specify deposit return, move-out date, and mutual release language before payment—have advocate review if possible before signing.

Third-party rent payment from charities or family may cure nonpayment if paid within notice window—coordinate payment timing with landlord and get receipt showing rent application not fees only.

Military SCRA may stay civil proceedings including eviction for active duty—notify court and landlord with orders copy if applicable.

Seasonal farmworker housing and employer-provided housing involve worker protection overlays—route specialized farmworker legal services when applicable.

After eviction judgment, record sealing or expungement statutes may help future housing applications—verify current Oregon legislation with advocate after case concludes.

Utility shutoff after move-out or during eviction may be landlord retaliation if done to pressure departure before court order—document separately from nonpayment claim.
